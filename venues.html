For venues

A café, a library, a museum, a council square. If you offer Wi-Fi and want people to find it, you will be able to claim the network, prove you control it, and decide what is published about it.

You cannot do this yet Venue sharing is phase P4, targeted around April 2027. The API is not live, there is no form, and nobody at this project will contact you asking you to sign up. This page describes what is planned, so that you can object to it while it is still cheap to change.

What you get out of it

  • Your network is shown at its actual position, not somewhere on the block.
  • Your venue's name is shown, so people know whose Wi-Fi it is.
  • You can publish the password, if there is one, so that joining is one tap instead of asking at the counter.
  • You can take it all back down. A network its owner withdraws is unpublished.

Read this before you decide

Verifying makes your venue's exact position and hardware addresses open data.

An unclaimed network is published deliberately vaguely: a name inside a cell about 150 m across, with no hardware address. Claiming a network removes that blurring on purpose, because being found exactly is the point of claiming.

What becomes public, under an open licence that allows anyone to copy, redistribute and build on it commercially:

  • The exact position of your access points, written to 5 decimal places and published with a stated precision of 10 m.
  • The hardware address (BSSID) of every access point you list.
  • Your venue's name and kind.
  • The password, if and only if you mark it public.

Once it is in a published file, other people have copies. Withdrawing removes it from the next dump; it does not reach the copies. Do not claim a network at a private address, and do not claim one that carries anything you would not put on a public map.

If that is not what you want, do nothing. An unclaimed network stays blurred, and you can also keep it out entirely.

How verification will work

The proof is that you can change the network's name. That is something a person who controls the access point can do and a passer-by cannot.

  1. Start the claim. You give the network name, the venue name and kind, the hardware addresses you want covered, and whether the password should be published. An email address is optional and is used only to send you the code and to reach you about the claim. It is never published.
  2. Get a challenge code. The server issues a short code, like ws-4f2a9c, which expires.
  3. Append it to the network name. Bar Centrale becomes Bar Centrale ws-4f2a9c. Save it on the router. People already connected may have to rejoin.
  4. Confirm on site. An app on the premises sees the renamed network and confirms it, together with the hardware address it is broadcasting from. That ties the claim to real hardware in the building.
  5. Put the name back. Once verified, the network is marked owner-verified and the code is no longer needed.

The confirmation step is why an app matters: somebody has to be standing in the building to see the renamed network on air. The apps do not exist yet, which is why this whole flow does not either.

Questions a venue usually asks

Do we have to publish the password?
No. You can list the network without it, which tells people the Wi-Fi exists and that they should ask. That choice is a field in the claim, not an afterthought.
What if we use a sign-in page?
That is recorded as a captive portal, so people know before they walk in that joining will involve a page and possibly a code.
Can somebody else claim our network?
Not without changing its name on our router, which needs access to it. That is the whole point of the challenge.
Do we have to keep the code in the name?
No. It is needed only for the confirmation, then you put the name back.
Can we change our mind?
Yes. A network withdrawn by its owner is unpublished. Copies of earlier published files are outside anyone's control, which is worth weighing before you start, not after.
Will you charge for this, or show our venue as an advert?
No. There is no payment, no ranking and no advertising anywhere in the design. The data is open and anyone can use it, including people who compete with us.
Is there a badge or a sticker for the window?
Not yet, and we would rather not invent one before there is anything to put on it.

Councils, libraries and anyone with a lot of access points

The claim format takes a list of hardware addresses, so one claim can cover every access point in a building or a square. The flow above is still per-claim and still needs somebody on site with an app. If you run public Wi-Fi at scale and already hold an authoritative list of your access points, that is a different and better import path, and it does not exist yet. It is worth an issue on spec before it gets designed without you.

What we will never ask you for

  • Your administrator password, or access to your router.
  • Payment.
  • Anything about the people using your Wi-Fi. The project collects nothing about them, and would not know what to do with it.
