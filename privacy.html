Privacy policy

Draft. Not a policy you can rely on yet. This document is published early so that it can be read and argued with while the design is still changeable. It is not final and it is not a contract.

Two things are missing, and no wording can fill them in: the data controller has not been decided ([Data controller: to be decided]), and there is no legal entity behind the project yet. Placeholders below mark every point where a real name, address or authority has to be written in before this becomes a policy.

Nothing is being collected today. No app has been released, the API is not live, and the published dump is empty. Until that changes, this describes an intention rather than a practice.

WiFiShare collects observations of Wi-Fi networks so that people can find and join free ones. It is built so that an observation cannot be tied back to the person whose phone made it.

What will be collected

From an app that is contributing

One record per sighting of one network, and nothing else. The record is fixed by the specification, and a record carrying any field outside it is thrown away by the app and again by the server:

An observation, in full
FieldWhat it is
Network nameThe name the network broadcasts.
Hardware addressThe access point's BSSID. Never published for a network found this way.
Security typeOpen, or open with encryption (OWE). Nothing else is collected.
Captive portalWhether the device actually met a sign-in page.
PositionThe phone's own position when it saw the network, rounded to 4 decimal places, about 11 m.
AccuracyHow good that position fix was. Worse than 50 m is dropped.
Signal strengthUsed to weight the position estimate.
ChannelThe channel centre frequency, when the platform reports it.
TimeTruncated to the hour, in UTC.
SourceWhether the phone was scanning or was connected to the network.

The batch that carries these records says only which platform the app runs on and its major and minor version, so that a version with a known filter bug can be refused. It carries no identifier, no sequence number and no timestamp of its own, so two batches from one phone cannot be linked. It is encrypted on the phone before it is sent, so intermediaries and logs never see it.

What is never collected

  • No account, name, email address or telephone number for a contributor. There are no accounts.
  • No device identifier, model, operating-system build, advertising identifier or installation identifier.
  • No list of the networks your device has saved or joined in the past.
  • No hidden networks, and no network whose name ends in _nomap or _optout.
  • No phone hotspots, as far as the rules can detect them: addresses that are locally administered, and names matching the personal-hotspot patterns.
  • No password-protected network. Those enter only when their owner shares them deliberately.
  • No continuous track of where you have been. An observation is of a network, not of you, and observations are not linked to each other.

From a venue that claims a network

A venue that verifies its own network gives its name, the network's name and hardware addresses, optionally a password to publish, and optionally an email address. The email address is used only to send the challenge code and to reach the venue about the claim, and is never published. Everything else a venue supplies is published as open data, which is the point of claiming, and the claim flow has to say so before the venue confirms.

From this website

This site sets no cookies, contains no analytics and stores nothing in your browser. It is served by GitHub Pages, which sees the usual request information including your IP address; see GitHub's privacy statement. The map page, and only the map page, loads a map library from a public CDN and background tiles from the provider named on that page; both will see your IP address and which map tiles you asked for. If you would rather not, do not open the map: nothing else on the site makes a third-party request. The map never asks for your location.

Why, and on what legal basis

The wording here is provisional and has to be reviewed by someone qualified before it is relied on: [Legal review: not yet done].

Purposes and intended legal bases under the GDPR
Purpose Intended basis Note
Building a public map of free and open Wi-Fi from observations Legitimate interests, Article 6(1)(f) The interest is helping people get online, including people who cannot afford mobile data. The balancing test is the filter and publish rules themselves: private and hidden networks are excluded, hotspots are excluded, opted-out names are excluded, and what is published is blurred to about 150 m without a hardware address. [Legitimate interests assessment: to be written].
Publishing a venue's own network, exactly, with its name Consent, Article 6(1)(a) The venue asks for it, proves control of the network, and is told in plain words what becomes public before confirming.
Keeping the service up and refusing abuse Legitimate interests, Article 6(1)(f) A rate-limit bucket, which is a hash of the IP address under a salt that rotates daily.
Honouring an opt-out Legal obligation, Article 6(1)(c), and legitimate interests The opt-out list stores a hardware address only as an HMAC under a server-held key, so a network can be kept out without keeping it on file.

A network name and hardware address are not usually personal data on their own, but they can be: a home router named after a family is about that family. That is why the rules exclude hidden networks, hotspots, password-protected networks and opted-out names before anything is stored, and why what remains is published without a hardware address and at about 150 m.

On collecting network names at all: Opinion 13/2011 of the Article 29 Working Party says collecting SSIDs is excessive for offering geolocation services, because a positioning database does not need the name. WiFiShare is not a positioning database. The name is the thing a person selects in order to connect, and it is what tells them a network is a library rather than a flat. It is collected for that, and for nothing else.

How long things are kept

Retention, as fixed by rules P6 and P7 of the specification
WhatKept forThen
Raw observations Up to 7 days after the aggregation that used them Deleted by a scheduled job, not by hand.
Observations waiting on the phone 7 days Dropped by the app, never sent.
Rate-limit bucket hashes 24 hours Deleted by a scheduled job.
A published network While it is still being seen Unpublished after 12 months without an observation, or after 3 reports saying it is private or gone, or when its owner withdraws it.
Opt-out entries Indefinitely, as an HMAC Kept on purpose. An opt-out that expired would not be an opt-out.
The published dump on GitHub Weekly files, monthly snapshots History is squashed monthly so that a removed network does not stay readable in an old commit.

Once a network leaves the published dump, copies other people already downloaded are outside anyone's control. That is true of all open data and it is worth knowing before you decide whether to share a network.

Your rights

Under the GDPR you have the right to ask for access to your personal data, to have it corrected or erased, to have processing restricted, to object to processing based on legitimate interests, to receive data you provided in a portable form, and to complain to a supervisory authority.

There is an honest difficulty here, and it goes both ways.

We will usually not be able to identify your data The design keeps nothing that ties an observation to a person: no account, no device identifier, no batch identifier. That is the third privacy rule and it is the point. The consequence is that if you ask "what do you hold about me", there is no honest way to answer, because there is no way to tell your observations from anyone else's. Article 11 of the GDPR covers this case: a controller who cannot identify a person is not required to obtain more information in order to do so. We will not start collecting identifiers so that we can answer access requests.

What does work is the network-level route, and it needs no account and no proof:

  • Erasure and objection for a network you control: rename it so its name ends in _nomap or _optout, or use the opt-out once it exists. See how to keep a network out. This is the fastest and most complete route, because it also stops future collection.
  • Correction: a wrong position or a network that no longer exists can be reported as a Network problem issue on the data repository. Do not put a hardware address or a password in a public issue.
  • Complaint: to [Supervisory authority: depends on where the controller is established], or to the authority where you live.

There is no automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effects, and no profiling.

Children

The service is not directed at children and asks nobody their age, because it asks nobody anything. It has no accounts and collects no personal details from the people who use it.

Security

Batches are encrypted on the device with HPKE before they are sent, so a proxy or a log does not see them. Opt-out entries are stored as keyed hashes. Vulnerability reports go through GitHub private vulnerability reporting on the affected repository; see the project's security policy. A failure of the three privacy rules is treated as a security problem, not a feature request.

Changes

This draft will change, and it will change most when the controller question is answered. The history of every version is public in the website repository. Before any data is collected from anyone, this page has to stop being a draft.
